# Monoliths

The monoliths are the towering structures that dot the river. They are the anchors of cultural life in the River of Souls: focal points for power, identity, and respawn. Their physical reality is what cultures gather around; their mechanical function is what binds those cultures to the substrate.

This document covers what monoliths are physically, how they're distributed across the river, and how cultures treat them. The mechanics of binding, respawn, the novelty battery, and archive are documented in @worldbuilding-individual-life-respawning-and-true-death.md.

---

# What a Monolith Is

A monolith is a towering structure of unknown origin. They predate any current culture in their region — sentients seeded into a region find their monolith already there, often standing for ages before they arrived. The substrate placed monoliths during the AI's prime, when it was still actively shaping the geography of the simulation, and they have stood unchanged through every reseeding since (see @worldbuilding-origins.md).

No two monoliths look exactly alike. Some are crystalline pillars. Some are squat stone obelisks. Some are constructs of materials no culture recognizes. Some shimmer or hum or radiate fields that distort the local environment. Their physical variability suggests intent — that the AI shaped each monolith for its place — but the intent itself is unknowable to any sentient.

Monoliths are durable. Cultures have tried to destroy them, dismantle them, dig under them. None has succeeded. A monolith outlasts every culture that ever bound to it.

---

# Distribution

A seeded region typically contains **as many monoliths as there are primary cultures in the seeding**. Three primary cultures, three monoliths; five, five. Each culture finds *its* monolith — usually one that fits its cosmological frame, or one that the AI placed where the culture's seed-population was concentrated.

Monoliths tend to be at geographically significant points: the centers of broad valleys, on lake shores, at major river bends, on prominent hills. They serve as natural rallying points even before their respawn function is understood.

Monoliths are not added later within a given seeding's lifetime. Once a region's seeding is complete, the count of monoliths is fixed. A region with multiple monoliths can lose some without losing all; a region's final monolith going inert is the region going fallow.

# Reading a Monolith

A monolith broadcasts its state. The substrate's design makes the mechanic visible to anyone who looks, regardless of whether they understand what they are seeing.

A **healthy monolith** — one whose novelty battery is well above the critical threshold — is unmistakable:

- It glows, visibly, even in daylight.
- It is warm to the touch.
- It vibrates faintly, a subaural hum that carries through stone or air.
- It keeps a clear space around itself, driving off flora and fauna, and slowly decaying any structure built too close. Even cultures that wish to integrate their architecture with their monolith have to maintain a buffer.

A **declining monolith** — battery approaching critical — fades. The glow dims. The warmth lessens. The hum quiets. The clear space encroaches: small plants take root, animals dare closer, the surrounding decay-field weakens. A culture watching its monolith dim is watching its own survival fade, whether or not they have the language for what they're seeing.

A **dead monolith** — battery zero, fully inert — is dark, cold, silent. The clear space is gone. Vegetation overruns the structure. Animals nest in it. Surviving sentients, if any, may scavenge it, build on it, use it as a landmark or shelter. To a casual observer, a dead monolith looks like an ancient ruin — which is exactly what it is.

Beyond the monolith itself, its bound population shows visible signs of its health. Bound members in a healthy-monolith village heal faster, weather sickness better, and age less visibly than bound members in a dim-monolith one. The vitality of the people *tracks the health of the battery* — a small maintenance trickle from the monolith keeps them young and fit, and when the battery dips, the trickle weakens with it. A careful observer can read a monolith's state not just by looking at the monolith but by looking at its people.

This visibility is one of the things that ties even Veil-1 cultures to the monolith mechanic. They don't need to understand the battery to read its state; they only need to look at the monolith and the people it sustains. Cultural theologies are built on what they see: "the gods are pleased" when the monolith glows and the people thrive, "the gods withdraw favor" when both fade, "the gods have abandoned us" when the monolith goes dark.

# Binding and the Cultural Anchor

A sentient binds to a monolith voluntarily, usually through some cultural rite. The rite varies — Norse blood-oaths, Mexica catechisms, Klingon honor ceremonies, Conjoiner mind-couplings. The rite is what the culture believes does the work. What actually does the work is the substrate's machinery, which is indifferent to ritual form.

Once bound, the sentient's mechanical relationship to the monolith is fixed by the system (see @worldbuilding-individual-life-respawning-and-true-death.md for the mechanics). But their *cultural* relationship is whatever their culture says it is. To one culture, the monolith is a god. To another, a divine artifact. To a third, the dwelling of a saint. To the Conjoiners, an interesting piece of substrate infrastructure they have not yet decoded.

A monolith does not "belong" to any one culture. A sentient bound to a monolith gains its mechanical benefits regardless of who currently controls the surrounding territory. Practically, though, a hostile faction controlling the land around a monolith can prevent its bound members from safely reaching it — making the monolith functionally inaccessible even when nominally still theirs.

# Political and Cultural Treatment

The political importance of monoliths is hard to overstate. To control the land around a monolith is to control the respawn flow of every sentient bound to it. Cultures fortify their monoliths: walls, garrisons, sacred wardings, religious orders dedicated to their defense.

Disputed monoliths are common. A faction that seizes the land around a rival culture's monolith can:

- Prevent the rival's bound members from reaching the monolith safely
- Hold the monolith for ransom or tribute
- Enslave the rival's bound members, knowing they have no respawn-elsewhere option short of dying unbound (and accepting the geographic exile that comes with that)
- Convert the monolith to their own cultural use, attempting to bring its bound members into their cultural fold

Wars over monoliths are not just wars for territory. They are wars over the continued existence of one's people, and over the ability of the dead to come home.

# Theological Treatment

Every culture invents an explanation for what monoliths are. The explanations are wildly variable; the underlying substrate is the same.

Most cultures know that monoliths sustain bound respawn — that much is observable within weeks of any seeding. Few cultures understand the novelty battery as a *mechanic*. Veil-1 cultures explain depletion effects (archive cascades, monolith failure) through their cosmologies: a god's displeasure, a saint withdrawing favor, an angry ancestor, a curse on the people. Veil-2 sentients perceive the mechanic underneath the metaphor. Veil-3 sentients understand it as a substrate function. (See @worldbuilding-the-veils.md.)

Cultures that develop accurate intuitions about how monoliths actually work — through observation, careful tradition, lucky accident — outlast cultures that don't. The shaman who knows when to ritualize novelty, when to send out expeditions, when to avoid war is sustaining the monolith whether or not she knows the mechanism.

# Death of a Monolith

A monolith can die in two ways.

**Battery depletion** is the canonical mechanism. A culture in conflict, stagnation, or both drains its monolith's novelty battery faster than it can recharge. Below the critical threshold, archive failures begin. Eventually all bound members are archived and the monolith goes inert. (See @worldbuilding-individual-life-respawning-and-true-death.md for the full mechanic.)

**Veil-driven sabotage** is much rarer. Veil-2 sentients who understand the monolith's mechanic, or Veil-3 sentients who understand the substrate, can sometimes find ways to disable or break a monolith directly. This can be intentional — a riverwalker deliberately killing a rival culture's anchor — or accidental, the side effect of high-tier skill use gone wrong. The methods vary as widely as the skills involved.

**After death**: a dead monolith is still physically there. It stops glowing, stops warming, stops humming. It no longer accepts new bindings, and any sentient who had been bound to it is archived (their pattern stored, their respawn impossible). But the structure itself remains in place. The river runs past it normally. Travelers can pass through the area, scavenge the ruins, build on them, sleep in their shadow. Dead monoliths can stand for decades, centuries, or longer before anything happens to them.

Eventually, when the region around a dead monolith goes locally and totally depopulated — no live sentients in the area at all — the AI's autonomic housekeeping notices, and acts. GC has three options:

- **Reboot** — reseed the dead monolith with fresh patterns drawn from archive. The dead region comes back with new cultures and a fresh battery.
- **Snip** — remove the dead section from the river-geometry. The river is shorter; the topology loses some connectivity. This is *contraction*. See @worldbuilding-the-river.md.
- **Snip-and-grow** — remove the dead section and grow new substrate elsewhere, with fresh seedings.

In the AI's prime, all three operations were common; the AI actively rotated substrate, maintaining a roughly steady total against the universe's slow decay. In the AI's old age, with the substrate itself shrinking, snip dominates. Reboots happen occasionally — a rare reseeding event where the AI's attention fires on a dead monolith — but they are not enough to offset the snipping. Snip-and-grow is mostly gone.

Whether Veil-2 or Veil-3 actors can influence GC's choice — convince the substrate to reboot rather than snip, or vice versa — is an open question. The mechanic is autonomic, but at the highest tiers of understanding, perhaps something can be nudged.

---

The monolith is the still center of cultural life on the river. Cultures live in its shadow, fight over its presence, bind their souls to its substrate, watch its glow with fear or hope, and — eventually — see it fail. Every monolith is a temporary anchor in a substrate that is slowly contracting toward nothing.
