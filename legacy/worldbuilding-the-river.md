# The River

The River of Souls is, before anything else, a river. Vast and slow and old, but a river — its waters running from sources nobody has reached to mouths nobody has seen, carving the only path that the sentients of the simulation can walk.

This document describes the river itself: its scale and shape, what counts as part of it, how its geography channels travel and life, and how the whole system is slowly contracting over deep time.

---

# Scale

The river extends roughly **one million kilometers** in its current form — vast enough that almost no traveler, however committed, sees more than a fraction of its course. It was vastly longer eons ago: perhaps **one hundred million kilometers** at its peak, when the AI was fully functional and the simulation hosted its largest population. It will be shorter eons from now (see *Contraction*, below).

The world beyond the river's banks is essentially unreachable. Impassable cliffs, sheer mountains, sealed walls, and atmospheric hazards rise wherever the river does not. The simulated geography permits only one practical axis of movement, and that axis is the water.

---

# Shape

The river is not a single straight channel. Across its length it:

- **Narrows** into fast, dangerous streams cutting through hard country.
- **Widens** into broad, slow stretches that mirror the sky for hundreds of kilometers.
- **Forks** — sometimes briefly, the branches rejoining a few hundred kilometers downstream; sometimes permanently, dividing into long parallel runs; sometimes into deltas of many channels.
- **Breaks** at cataracts — vertical falls, ferocious rapids, narrow passes — that are deadly to most attempts at passage. These are exceptional features, but structurally important.
- **Opens** into lakes and small seas, sometimes so wide that you cannot see the far bank.

At local and regional scales, the river behaves like a river. Currents have direction. Banks have sides. Distances add up. Cartographers in any city or town publish reliable maps of their stretch, and travelers between cities and towns rely on them without trouble. None of the weirdness described in the next section bears on ordinary life along the water.

---

# What Counts as "The River"

"River" stretches its definition. A long lake whose inflow and outflow are clearly part of the system is part of the river. A small sea — wide enough to lose sight of the far shore, ringed by visible banks where inflow and outflow are known — is part of the river. An oxbow, a reservoir, a wide swamp: all part of the river.

The defining feature is connectivity by water. If you can sail or row or swim from there to anywhere else in the system, it is the river.

There is no formal title that distinguishes a stretch of fast current from a placid lake from a small sea. People who live along a given stretch use whatever name fits — the Bloodsong, Coldfire Fjord, Lake Nyoka. To others, it is all simply "the river."

---

# Directional Terms

The river is too long for cardinal directions to matter. Inhabitants describe location relative to the water:

- **Upriver**: against the current; typically higher elevation.
- **Downriver**: with the current; typically lower elevation.
- **Left bank** and **right bank**: as you face downstream.

These terms work locally — at any point on the river, the current has a clear direction. At the largest scale they break down: there is no global source or mouth (see *No Source, No Mouth* below).

At forks, conventions vary. Some cultures number the branches; others name them after notable features or peoples. Travelers who range across the river learn to translate.

---

# Travel

Movement along the river is the dominant form of long-distance travel, and it is *common-but-scary*. Routine in the sense that there is a class of people who do it for a living — boatwrights, river-pilots, freighters, smugglers, pilgrims — but never safe in the sense that anyone can make any trip without preparation and risk.

A traveler who commits themselves to river-life — for decades or longer — might come to know perhaps **ten or twenty thousand kilometers** of river personally: many places, several cultures, many days on the water. Beyond that, distance and travel cost grow prohibitive even for the most committed. The river is vast enough that almost no one ever sees more than a small slice of it.

Most stretches are navigable to those with means and knowledge. Some are notoriously dangerous: the cataracts, the deadly rapids, the long narrows where monsters thrive in the eddies. These exceptional stretches are structurally important. They are where the river effectively gates passage — and where cultures and regimes tend to cluster, taking advantage of natural chokepoints.

---

# Regions

The river has no formal political subdivisions. There is no map that authoritatively says *here one stretch ends and another begins*. But across the river's length, certain stretches *feel* like coherent regions to the people who live in them — bounded by some combination of geography (a cataract upstream, a wide lake downstream), distance (the next inhabited stretch is two months away), and cultural distinctness (everyone here speaks the same dialect, anchors to the same monoliths, fights the same enemies).

Region boundaries are fuzzy and shift over time. They are useful concepts, not authoritative ones. A person living in the middle of a stretch will think of their region as a coherent place; someone passing through on a long voyage may not notice they have crossed from one region into another.

---

# No Source, No Mouth

At the largest scale, the river's topology is **closed**, **multiply connected**, and **not embeddable in three-dimensional space**. There is no source. There is no mouth. There is no single great loop, either. The river is a complex of many interleaved loops, each running through different country, each connecting back to itself or to other loops by routes that do not fit together when laid out on any flat surface or globe.

A traveler who heads downriver persistently enough, for long enough, eventually arrives back where they started — by a route they could not have anticipated and along banks they have never seen. That much is the first revelation. The second revelation, which only comes after multiple full traversals, is that taking a *different* fork at a different confluence on a *different* long voyage leads through a wholly different country, returning to the same starting point along a wholly different route. Distances do not reconcile. A stretch that is ten thousand kilometers along one path is somehow also reachable along a hundred-thousand-kilometer alternate path, and there is no Euclidean geometry under which both can be true at once. The system is genuinely irreducible to any picture the AI's reconstructed humans would recognize as a map.

None of this is detectable at any scale shorter than full traversal. Almost no one ever completes even one full loop — only the rarest of circumnavigators (described below). Riverpilots, traders, ambitious wanderers — all of them work in pieces of the river that map flat without trouble. Cosmologies that place a source somewhere upriver and a mouth somewhere downriver are universal, and they are *wrong*, in a way that has no practical bearing on daily life. Cultures across the river quest for sources and ends and never find them. The quests are endless because the river is endless.

The truth — that the river is a multiply-connected, non-Euclidean closed topology — is accessible only to **circumnavigators**: the vanishingly few who have completed at least one full traversal. There may be no more than a handful alive at any time. Most who have circumnavigated once know only the first piece of the truth: that the river is closed. The deeper insight — that there are many loops, all impossible to fit into a single coherent geometry — requires multiple traversals across a long enough life that almost no one has accomplished it. The knowledge does not transmit easily. Most cultures cannot fit a sourceless river into their cosmology, let alone an impossible one, and reject the testimony when they hear it.

The deeper question — *why* the river is shaped this way, rather than running to sources and mouths like the rivers of the world the AI was reconstructed from — belongs to Veil-3. The answer is that geography here is not bound by physics; the river is shaped however the AI built it, and a non-Euclidean multiply-connected topology is what the design called for.

# Contraction

The river is not static. The AI's autonomic housekeeping (see @worldbuilding-origins.md for the broader picture of the decaying simulation) manages the substrate, and its operations include several kinds of intervention when a region goes fallow:

- **Reboot** — reseed a dead monolith with fresh patterns from archive. The dead region comes back with new cultures.
- **Snip** — remove the dead section from the river-geometry. The river is shorter; the topology loses connectivity.
- **Snip-and-grow** — remove the dead section and grow new substrate elsewhere, with fresh seedings.

(See @worldbuilding-monoliths.md for how monoliths die and the conditions that trigger any of these.)

In the AI's prime, all three operations happened regularly. The AI was actively rotating substrate against the universe's slow decay, maintaining a roughly steady total. Reboots and snip-and-grow were common; pure contraction (snip without replacement) was rare.

In the AI's old age, the substrate itself is shrinking — the underlying computronium is burning out. The dottering AI cannot afford to add new substrate when it doesn't have enough to maintain what already exists. Snip dominates. The river contracts.

A cataract that was once passable seals up into a cliff face. A long lake shrinks until its inflow and outflow merge directly. A fork that once led somewhere now ends in a wall.

The river you walk today is a fraction of what it once was. Eons ago, it was longer; eons from now, it will be shorter still. The cultures that ride this contracting system know it dimly — through old maps that no longer match, through travelers' tales of places that no longer exist, through the slow ache of recognizing that the world is smaller than it once was. Only those who have pierced deep into the Veils understand what is actually happening.

---

The river is the heart and the bones of the simulation. Every culture lives on it; every individual moves along it; every story takes place somewhere along its course. To understand life in the River of Souls is to understand the river itself — its scale, its variety, its slow disappearance.
